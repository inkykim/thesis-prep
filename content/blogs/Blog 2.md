
**Scholarly Article**

Summary

In "Modernity in South Korea: An Alternative Narrative," sociologist Hagen Koo argues against the conventional understanding of modernization in South Korea as a linear transformation. South Korea's modernization is characterized by its rapid industrialization and corresponding economic development, urbanization, and democratization. As such, this process is understood as a progression from tradition to modernity, with older social structures being replaced by new ones over time. However, Koo offers a new perspective, that Korean modernity emerged through social conflict, particularly between modernity and tradition, a more complex and two-sided dialogue than the aforementioned explanation.

Koo's main idea is that tradition does not disappear through modernization, as the linear explanation would imply. Instead, elements of history and tradition are rediscovered, questioned, reconstructed, and incorporated into contemporary society as part of the process of modernization. Thus tradition and modernity do not necessarily exist on two ends of a timeline: instead, they operate simultaneously. Older social relationships manifest in new forms within modern systems, and the two engage in competition that Koo places on the same timeline as tension between global influences and national identity.

Reflection

In the previous blog, I was particularly interested in the contradiction between Seoul's innovation and homogenous social culture. The earlier sources about compressed modernity described this mismatch as a result of two forces moving at different speeds, but Koo presents a fascinating perspective on whether this is a mismatch at all.

For instance, social media and climbing gyms are two examples I have thought about for my earlier blog post. Instagram is clearly structured around individuality, with my own profile showcasing my experiences and aesthetic. Yet my experience in Seoul has been that this infrastructure for individuality has consistently produced collective behavior. In climbing gyms, the same problems are filmed from the same angles and circulated through the same circles. Koo's paper frames this phenomenon as not a result of a progression, but the complex byproduct of dialogue between traditional social tendencies and the process of modernization. Rather than older collective tendencies surviving despite modernization, perhaps new systems and environments are providers of new ways for the tendencies to operate under.

This distinction is particularly interesting when I approach my thesis and how I think about Seoul from an architectural perspective. The question becomes less about going from building a new city for an old society, and more about how different social systems can coexist within one contemporary environment. Under the framework of this continuous reinforcement and dialogue between digital networks and social behaviors, I believe that architecture is not just something I can design as a reflection, or something adjacent to society, but one of the systems participating in the dialogue itself.

Citation

Koo, Hagen. “Modernity in South Korea: An Alternative Narrative.” _Thesis Eleven_ 57, no. 1 (1999): 53–64. [https://doi.org/10.1177/0725513699057000005](https://doi.org/10.1177/0725513699057000005).

**Freeform Reference**

Summary

In "Change, Tokyo! A Free, Loose, and Open City," architect Kengo Kuma and journalist Yumi Kiyono engage in a dialogue about Tokyo and its cycles of destruction, construction, and modernization. As the two discuss and question what kind of city must come next for society after a big reset such as the 2020 pandemic, Kuma specifically details his idea of "looseness" (as in the title) and a proposed shift from large architectural objects to smaller adaptable spaces that accommodate more flexible forms of city life.

Japan also went through a characteristic rapid modernization in its postwar period. Architecturally, this transformation was joined by a new school of urban design that could keep up with this large scale, leading to megastructures, stadiums, and other large projects that participated in the construction of a new modern Japan. Kuma is an architect that follows this period and thus conducts work upon the Tokyo that is the result of this process, but his vision is completely different: he places emphasis on openness and adaptability, with small and material-sensitive designs that do not impose on the actual usage of the space.

Reflection

I thought the comment suggestion about thinking about Japan was a great idea to provide a parallel perspective on another city characterized by rapid modernization, and my first thought was to look back at this book, which I read on the plane as I moved to work in Tokyo this summer. I've always known and respected Kuma as an architect that works with particular sensitivity to social conditions. Now as I operate in the scope of my thesis, his writing made me think again about my instinct to respond to my observations of Seoul's social conditions by designing a new type of social space.

The problem I observe in Seoul is that social behavior is increasingly structured by overlapping physical and digital systems. Kuma describes the postwar architecture movement in Japan as one designed to follow the scaling of the city, but with the effect of creating large object-like spaces and rigid infrastructure for city life. An fascinating thought is that these are conceptually the same issue, architecture being the vessel for the same social tension in Tokyo. Then, logically, approaching design as another system to create and impose only adds to the problem: I must approach architecture through subtraction.

Particularly, I have been thinking about nightlife. A contemporary club exists simultaneously as a physical and digital space, because videos and photos from a night out become the version of the experience that circulate on the social circles that exist online. Even during the experience, there exists an awareness of another audience, thus the experience becomes a performance of experiencing. Now I imagine a room whether these elements have been deleted. Crowds are circulated through word of mouth, and phones are not allowed on the dance floor. Architecturally, the design is a removal, not an addition. However, now a connection in the cycle of physical/digital performance has been severed, and the space houses truly visceral experience. These have been the earliest thoughts in my head, but it is interesting to draw these comparisons with Kuma's writings about Tokyo.

Citation

Kuma, Kengo, and Yumi Kiyono. _変われ！東京 自由で、ゆるくて、閉じない都市_ (Change, Tokyo! A Free, Loose, and Open City). Tokyo: Shueisha, 2020.
