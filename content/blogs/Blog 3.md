
**Scholarly Article**

Summary

In "Cafe as a Third Place and Modern Habits of the Heart," Sang-In Jun and Meeyoung Kim write about the cafe as a social space above a simple commercial establishment. In particular, they reference Ray Oldenburg's original concept of the "third space," a place of gathering outside the two dominant spaces of human life: home and work. Due to the third space's distinct separation from the two main formal spaces, these spaces can provide an informal setting where even strangers can develop conversations, relationships, and participate in a shared public life. In the article, the authors argue that these interactions contribute to the development of modern social habits, including individualism and citizenship.

Particularly, in the context of contemporary Korea, where cafes have proliferated at a rapid and widespread scale, the authors raise the question of whether the abundance of cafes actually represents a correlation with the embedding of contemporary social habits in everyday Korean life. The physical space of a cafe is an architectural and commercial typology, but the relationship between the presence of a cafe and the manifestations of human behavior that occur within them is a separate concept, more aligned with social studies.

Reflection

In previous blogs, I have looked at spaces in Seoul that could be considered "third spaces," such as cafes, bars, climbing gyms, and many more semi-public and interactive spaces that are densely distributed throughout the city. While Jun and Kim explain that cafes are examples of spaces that have historically functioned alongside Oldenburg's definition of third spaces, they also raise the relevant question: is the fact that there are so many cafes in Seoul a direct representation of this healthy behavior? I am not convinced that the proliferation of such a space is necessarily the driver of the spontaneous social interaction that Oldenburg originally described.

I believe that Seoul instead demonstrates that the physical development of third spaces and their social conditions (that originally defined them) are actually two distinct characteristics within one piece of architectural grammar. Many cafes in Seoul are optimized towards consumption, aesthetics, and documentation. Visiting a trendy cafe, photographing its interior and food, and posting about it online allows the cafe to be crowded and visually lively, but this does not necessarily mean that the cafe is functioning as a third space. It is not sufficient to simply build a cafe and call it a third space: a more thoughtful architectural dialogue is necessary to orient the building towards human interaction.

This distinction is one I hope to approach with my thesis design. I believe that the design of a building is only the first step in generating a framework for people to occupy the space, but this cannot guarantee that those people interact. I am increasingly become more interested in a lens or detail-oriented approach to architecture that can move in a more social direction.

Citation

Jun, Sang-In, and Meeyoung Kim. “Cafe as a Third Place and Modern Habits of the Heart.” _Studies in Urban Humanities_, 2021.

**Freeform Reference**

Summary

In "Asia's Electronic Music Scenes: Seoul, Tokyo, Shanghai, Bangkok," Seoul-based writer and DJ Matthew Clement compares electronic music scenes across several asian cities including Seoul and Tokyo. Clement explains that Tokyo "built the institution before the scene arrived" while Seoul "built the scene before the institution existed." 

As such, Tokyo's nightlife includes storied venues and attentive listening and dancing culture that persisted through an institutional memory. In turn, new names in the DJ scene or new types of events and experiences may develop, but still be able to maintain a maturity in infrastructure. In contrast, Seoul's scene is newer and smaller, more dependent on energy and adaptability. Many clubs and DJs are not yet established institutions, and focus heavily on social media to promote events, creating a looser social network in the digital realm to complement the incomplete physical framework.

Reflection

I had previously thinking about Seoul through a curiosity for social spaces that do not actually produce deeper forms of collective life. Nightlife seems to be an intense example of this question. The article was particularly interesting to read because it shows that the experience of nightlife is heavily shaped by not just the music and the venue but the behavior of the crowd and the platform of communication, which is also highly correlated to policy, regulation, and design at an urban scale. 

In particular, the contrast between Seoul and Tokyo, one that I explored also in my previous blog post, is interesting because the two cities have both similarities and culture and stark differences in their modern history. Tokyo's scene seems to be supported by institution and reputation, and an audience trained to listen and dance. Music rooms may not be designed to be images, but purely to make people stay in the atmosphere of the music. Seoul instead seems to have energy before structure, which leads to an especially lively scene. However, it also raises the architectural question of what it means to design a nightclub in Seoul that introduces continuity, bringing the structured atmosphere of mutual understanding and listening without feeling like a rigid restriction on the current scene.

This article also confirms the thoughts I've been having with the other sources related to social media, cafes, or third spaces, which is that the design problem I want to approach is not just aesthetic. Before I design what a Seoul nightclub should look like, I must study and determine what kinds of behavior it should support. Because of the nature of the city and its people, it would be unproductive of me to design a nightclub that only I would enjoy (perhaps this disagrees with my original ideas, radical visions, and reimaginations of nightlife as a whole). I want to think more about what could truly work with the people of Seoul, attention over documentation, stability over novelty, somewhere in between Seoul and Tokyo, the past and the future.

Citation

Clement, Matthew. “Asia’s Electronic Music Scenes: Seoul, Tokyo, Shanghai, Bangkok.” _The DJ Diaries_, July 9, 2026.
