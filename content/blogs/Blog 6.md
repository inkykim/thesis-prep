
**Scholarly Article**

Summary

In *Polyvalence: The Competence of Form and Space with Regard to Different Interpretations,* renowned architect Herman Hertzberger introduces a new paradigm for the definition of adaptability in architecture, challenging the assumption that adaptation requires conventional "flexibility." The intention of flexible architecture is in reconfiguration (whether the space is physically adjustable, or is designed to be neutral) for the purpose of accommodating more than one program. However, Hertzberger argues that this intention results in a loss of identity, such that the architecture, instead of accommodating everything, is suited to nothing.

Hertzberger's original contribution in the paper is an alternate criteria which he calls "polyvalence," a measure of how architecture can accommodate multiple interpretations without necessarily undergoing a change in definition. Accordingly, the gap that invites design is not between rigidity and flexibility, but between architecture that prescribes specific behavior and architecture that invites interpretation. This means that architects need not design spaces for a number of predetermined functions or attempt to create an all-encompassing utopian solution, but simply introduce qualities to the form that allow occupants to explore their usage. Consequently, Hertzberger argues that the architect's responsibility is not to relinquish formality or intention for the purpose of accommodation. Meaningful and recognizable architecture does not mean that their significance is immune to evolution through usage.

Reflection

In my previous blog, I explored the conceptual, unbuilt Fun Palace and a successful contemporary precedent M.N. Roy, and considered a seemingly contradictory tension in this comparison. The Fun Palace was designed to be fueled by uncertainty through its adaptive framework, while M.N. Roy was a highly exclusive and curated project working within rigid restrictions of the existing site. However, I ultimately believed that M.N. Roy was more successful in cultivating an authentic social atmosphere. Hertzberger's reframing argument offers insight into where this opposition originates. Perhaps architectural freedom is not as much correlated on how much certainty the architect constructs, but how the resulting architecture can accommodate experience.

In particular, I am very interested in this distinction's relevance to the nightclub, which is a unique space in that the patterns of occupation can be continually reformed through changes in music, movement, or crowd. As such, it is impossible for architectural design alone to anticipate and configure towards all such possible experiences. However, as evident from M.N. Roy, these experiences are undoubtedly influenced by spatial decisions, meaning that design choices such as double heights, thresholds, and material choices must also take part in encouraging interaction, even if they do not explicitly engage in them. This also raises the question about the distinction between curation and prescription. While the intent of curation becomes problematic when it infringes on the agency of the occupants and determines how they should behave, the deliberate curation of architectural form seems to be compelling and even necessary for proper function.

Citation

Hertzberger, Herman. “Polyvalence: The Competence of Form and Space with Regard to Different Interpretations.” _Architectural Design_ 84, no. 5 (2014): 106–113. [https://doi.org/10.1002/ad.1816](https://doi.org/10.1002/ad.1816).

Sujoldang is a private residence in Seoul, designed for art historian Yoo Hong-jun by architect Seung H-Sang and completed in 1992. "Sujoldang" is a portmanteau of Chinese-Korean characters meaning "humble house," and the intent of the design is to encourage contemplation and movement, and constantly assess the relationship between architecture and daily life. Seung makes deliberate choices, at times ignoring conventional notions of comfort and efficiency, to make life in the house never sedentary, with long-winded paths and use of empty space in central areas. 

Accompanying the project is an essay from Seung that sharply critiques contemporary Korean residential architecture, arguing that rapid economic development and the disparity gap has led architecture to serve grandeur and appearance rather than the experience of the inhabitants. In particular, Seung points out that the prioritization of property value is correlated with a public view of houses as symbols of status, which then again leads to design choices informed by expensive materials and the separation of private property from its surroundings. Seung's design of Sujoldang attempts to critique and escape these social norms and rediscover the values of modest and communal responsibility, which was originally associated with traditional Korean architecture. The garden in the middle is inspired by historic Korean forms, and the complicated program is modeled around the collective work of residents, at the scale of the house and the scale of the city.

Reflection

I'm fascinated by this precedent because Seung's writing and intentions seem highly correlated with my initial thoughts that motivated the thesis topic. My earliest blog posts about Seoul's compressed modernity have explored the sociological results of rapid development. Seung's critique takes a similar idea and identifies a specific phenomena in residential architecture, where the house itself has prioritized the image of a desirable lifestyle rather than actually inhabiting it.

Another aspect of Seung's design that I want to reflect on is the way traditional Korean architecture might inform my own design approach in a way that does not simply "reference" historical precedents or aesthetics. Sujoldang achieves this through its spatial organization, taking inspiration from rather than literally reproducing traditional form. In particular, the use of courtyards (very common in traditional Korean architecture) has been reinterpreted as central voids in Sujoldang. Going back to Kengo Kuma's discussion of Tokyo, as well as the value of "subtraction" I have discussed in previous blog posts, I feel that, as I try to offer an escape from the present and future, drawing from the past is not just a decision but a necessary element of my design.

Finally, Seung's argument and questioning of functionality is interesting and relevant to my research. Contemporary architecture often prioritizes efficiency, treating circulation and transitional spaces as necessities that must be minimized. Commercial architecture takes this a step further, maximizing circulation and profit such as in the digitally hyper-optimized precedents I initially explored. As with M.N. Roy, Sujoldang's program seems to include a large amount of control from the architect, contrary to more radical flexibility as seen in Fun Palace or Space Electronica. However, through the careful curation of a complex program, Seung's work does ultimately encourage the inhabitant to think more about the space around him during the moments he must pass through incidental spaces. This is a fascinating manifestation of agency and flexibility unlike all of my previous precedents, where the existence of uninvited moments gives rise to meaningful experiences initiated purely by the occupant.
