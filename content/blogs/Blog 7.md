
**Scholarly Article**

Summary

In “Lina Bo Bardi and the SESC Pompéia Leisure Center: A Case Study in Adaptive Reuse,” Lara Damabi discusses architect Lina Bo Bardi's project in transforming an abandoned factory in São Paulo into a cultural and recreational center between 1977 and 1986. The project follows principles of preservation and subtraction: rather than demolishing the existing factory, Bo Bardi preserved the industrial frame and mostly introduced new interior programs that supported the surrounding community.

In an early visit to the site Bo Bardi saw families relaxing, children playing, and other recreational activities and community gatherings in the factory. The abandoned complex had already been informally occupied by the local community before its renovation. Thus, Bo Bardi's architecture was guided significantly by these very spontaneous activities, instead of her own principles that she wanted to project.

The project retains the original factory sheds and internal street and introduces programmed spaces such as libraries, workshops, and exhibition rooms. A main "activities pavilion" accommodates multiple programs with an open spatial arrangement that freely facilitates group activities such as reading and exhibition viewing. In the article, Damabi argues that Bo Bardi's approach challenged the convention for architectural "authorship" by not overwriting, but respecting and facilitating the culture and society that was already organically present in the existing environment.

Reflection

I think Damabi's reflection of Bo Bardi's definition of authorship is particularly relevant to my thesis, as well as the fact that the site already had existing activities emerge after it was abandoned and before it was renovated. In my previous blog I explored the definition of "polyvalence," a measure of how architecture can accommodate multiple interpretations without necessarily undergoing a change in definition. With this project, this applies to both the subtractive nature of the architectural design, but also the community that had naturally emerged before the architect's interference. The architecture did not create this social activity but provided the conditions for it to continue and thrive. 

On the other hand, I think there is an interesting duality in Bo Bardi's design, because although the project is designed for spontaneous social activity, its architecture remains extremely deliberate. The arrangement of furniture and circulation in communal spaces and the well-defined functionality of each room and pavilion block all meticulously reflect one specific ideology of how people should interact with each other and the space. In that sense, the architect indeed has exercised their authorship, just in a manner that encourages a certain form of social participation.

We thus circle back again to the question of how architectural intention influences authentic social agency, the first question I began investigating with the thesis. My goal is to design a space that enables social interaction and cultural catharsis without prescribing the experience of its occupants. I have explored how architects that attempt to define specific programs do not achieve this, but in SESC Pompéia's case the intention behind that attempt precisely follows the philosophy I desire. I'm interested to look further into this precedent and find more justification into how Bo Bardi's design succeeds in facilitating interaction and fails in creating true polyvalence.

**Freeform Reference**

Summary

In "Curates: De School," British dance and DJ magazine Mixmag interviews the team behind De School, a renovation project in Amsterdam that converted a technical school into a nightclub in 2016. Rather than renovating the existing building structurally or extensively, the team's design philosophy was focused on retaining the school's original architecture while introducing new cultural programs, including a nightclub, restaurant, cafe, gym, gallery, concert venue, and outdoor garden. For instance, the nightclub took the place of a former bicycle garage in the basement; specific architectural details such as the low ceilings, concrete construction, and minimal lighting setup were not replaced, but reworked to emphasize an intimate atmosphere for the nightclub.

The interview is also focused on the team's process behind the project, which included local volunteers contributing to renovation work and local musicians participating in early programming. In that sense, the team intended De School to operate as a cultural destination beyond just a nightclub. For that purpose, the existing architectural structure was taken to be the framework.

Reflection

As with previous precedents I've explored on subtractive architecture, De School is interesting to me as it was able to implement a completely different cultural identity without losing its architectural character. Even specific things like the original classrooms and hallways, which were designed around education, were appropriated for the almost polar opposite programming of a nightclub without destroying the design framework. 

One particularly interesting relationship here is that between the nightclub programming and the other spaces throughout the building. The nightclub space (the basement) is well fit and specifically designed for being a dance room, and is successful at identifying as such. However, De School also includes so many other programs (cafe, gym, gallery, etc.) around the building, each guided by the framework of the architecture. A visitor could be dancing in one room, then go down the hallway into a different classroom and view some art. This reflects the particularly diverse programming of an actual school, the original building, where completely different subjects or programs can occur throughout a student's day in the same building.

I believe this project also raises the same question as the SESC Leisure Center did, about the architect's deliberate programming: the basement was very particularly designed to be a nightclub, as was each other program in the space. However, as the architectural framework respects the original building and the circulation of the space, these different experiences are able to create authentic spatial flexibility and social agency as a visitor explores the building. This paradigm of not just designing a building but designing relationships between different spaces and activities is one I would love to explore in my design process as I pursue how architecture can enable my nightclub's visitors to go about their night on their own terms rather than a prescribed experience.
