
**Scholarly Article**

Summary

In "Night-clubs et discothèques: visions d'architecture," Carlotta Darò discusses the nightclub as an important site of architectural experimentation in the 1960s and 1970s. Specifically, Darò characterizes nightclubs not just as venues for entertainment, but "laboratories" for avant-garde architects interested in the relationships between architecture, technology, and social behavior. This is because of the nightclub's open program; its interior did not need to fit a rigid set of rules for specific activities, but depended highly on the music being played and the way people interacted with the space.

One particular focus of the article is the "Piper" club that emerged in an Italian radical architecture movement. The movement included a prompt by Leonardo Savioli at the University of Florence for students to think about the nightclub as a "space of participation." The resulting projects emphasized flexibility and technological infrastructure rather than permanent form, especially with interior choices such as modular or portable furniture, partitions, and stages. Darò describes these projects as functioning less like a built "room" but more like a "machine" for sound and dance, where light and sound became a part of the architecture alongside walls and floors like materials themselves. The central argument of the article is thus a conceptual shift in understanding of architecture from a built form to a designed environment. With the radical Italian nightclubs, the student architectural projects did not feel like traditional studio projects ending in a finished object, but rather essays that established technical conditions through which experiences might emerge.

Reflection

In my previous blogs, I did more broad reading into social interaction, and only thought about nightclubs vaguely, primarily through their ability to encourage or discourage social interaction. This article is a more specific, architectural perspective on that topic. Instead of asking what a nightclub could look like, it asks what conditions the architecture should make possible.

I think the most crucial distinction in the article is the point about designing a space versus designing an environment. A conventional interior operates under a stable relationship between program and form, and this is how I've approached design in residential projects in past studios and work experiences. A stage is placed "here" so that people can stand "there" and thus circulation is defined. However, a nightclub is particularly interesting because its occupation changes continuously. Depending on the genre or personality of the artist/DJ, depending on the mood of the crowd or the ripeness of the night, the same physical space is perceived in completely different ways. 

As such, I am beginning to think more seriously about how incomplete the architecture itself can remain, such that the experience constitutes the continuous reconstruction of the architecture. Clearly, as the architect, I can think about lighting systems, circulations, and overall infrastructure and programming, but the experience is completed with participation.

Citation

Darò, Carlotta. “Night-clubs et discothèques: visions d’architecture” [“Nightclubs and Discotheques: Visions of Architecture”]. _Intermédialités / Intermediality_, no. 14, 2009, pp. 85–103. [https://doi.org/10.7202/044411ar](https://doi.org/10.7202/044411ar).

**Freeform Reference**

Summary

In "A New Film Demonstrates Why Gruppo 9999, an Architecture Collective from Florence, Remains Relevant Today," Italian architect Alessandro Bava presents a specific example of the experimental nightclub culture described in the scholarly article. Gruppo 9999 is a group of Italian artists and architects, known for designing Space Electronic, an experimental club that opened in Florence in 1969. The design of the club was particularly inspired by Gruppo 9999's experiences in the United States such as the Electric Circus and Andy Warhol's Exploding Plastic Inevitable, which combined music, performance, art, and technology within a single constantly changing space.

One unique feature about Space Electronic is that while it opened as a nightclub, it was never designed to be exclusively a nightclub. In 1970, the space also operated during the day as an architecture studio, including taught classes, installations, and performances. In one event in 1971 called "Life, Death, and Miracles of Architecture," the dance floor of the nightclub was turned into a landscape with grass, trees, and a pond for an architectural experience event. Such experiences demonstrate that Space Electronic is an example of architecture that was not defined as a space containing an arrangement. Instead, its existence and identity was continuously shaped by the flexible usage of the space, where the events and people that the space held were able to constantly define new versions of the same nightclub.

Reflection

Space Electronic seems to me to be an extreme example of the idea of "flexibility" I mentioned for the scholarly article, as well as previous blog posts. I have historically understood the description of an architectural project as "flexible" (in my own projects and in describing existing spaces) in terms of things like movable partitions, furniture, or the choice of various programs. However, Gruppo 9999's work completely abandons even controlled gestures, with Space Electronic not simply switching between known functions but being left open to purposes that even the architects and the architecture could not completely anticipate.

The article made this point especially interesting to read and think about because many of the images of Space Electronic in the article depict temporary conditions rather than the architecture itself. Between the famous parachute, various visual projections, recycled furniture, and natural elements make it such that there is no consistent visual language I can associate with the architectural project, but rather a more nebulous and atmospheric overarching idea of human feelings and actions that happened within the space.

This idea is particularly interesting to me and feels relevant to the social research I've done in previous blog posts as well as the motivation about Seoul that I have mentioned before. I feel that a standard studio methodology to have approached the question of designing nightlife spaces for social interaction to design a visually distinct club with programs placed such that social behavior will follow. However, Gruppo 9999's work presents a completely opposite approach of allowing the character of the space to be reversely defined by the behavior that naturally occurs. I am now particularly curious about the architect's job under such a methodology. What decisions do the architect actually need to make here?

Citation

Bava, Alessandro. “A New Film Demonstrates Why Gruppo 9999, an Architecture Collective from Florence, Remains Relevant Today.” _The Architect’s Newspaper_, 14 June 2023.
