
**Scholarly Article**

Summary

In "The Fun Palace as Virtual Architecture: Cedric Price and the Practices of Indeterminacy," Stanley Mathews analyzes Cedric Price and Joan Littlewood's radical architecture project Fun Palace as an experimental environment for public interaction. The Fun Palace was a proposal for a flexible infrastructural framework, rather than a conventional building operating under a defined program, including spaces that could be rearranged according to the participation of its occupants. Movable, modular partitions and mechanical systems define Price and Littlewood's vision to replace the permanence of architectural monoliths with improvised environments.

The paper is particularly interesting as it places the project within the technological optimism of postwar Britain, with political and social attention to the emerging field of cybernetics (the study of activity in living organisms and machines). Working with cybernetician Gordon Pask, Price designed a system that could gather information about users' preference and simultaneously adapt the building itself to accommodate these demands. Here, the author Matthews questions this idea fundamentally. Although the Fun Palace intended to liberate individuals from prescribed forms of leisure, its methodology of algorithmic feedback was also highly susceptible to behavioral manipulation and social control.

Reflection

Particularly fascinating about the Fun Palace's radical intention is the potential contradiction between its purpose of individual agency and the rigid mechanisms through which such agency is facilitated. In my previous blog post about Space Electronic, I was interested in how architecture could encourage spontaneity without prescribing it. The Fun Palace is an extension of this idea, allowing occupants to influence not only the activities within a building but potentially the physical building itself. However, this raises the question of whether this constant feedback loop between the building and the occupants necessarily promotes freedom: adaptation to patterns and preferences may more likely result in simply the reinforcement of existing collective behavior. There is a striking parallel that can be drawn here with Seoul's contemporary culture, which I have written about in earlier blogs, where digital platforms act as new realms for collective behavior to flourish, but ultimately just reproduce homogenized patterns.

I also wonder whether architectural flexibility is the correct solution to facilitate social freedom. The Fun Palace intends for its occupants to become participants in its own design, but the underlying system seems to establish more rigid parameters than complete freeform experimentation like that of Gruppo 9999. In that sense, the architect has not necessarily relinquished authorship, but instead relocated it from his own design of the architecture to a defined system that governs it. This distinction makes me uncertain whether physical adaptability is truly an effective means of supporting spontaneity and participation. Perhaps the architect's responsibility is not in reconfiguration, but in accommodation, of behaviors not chosen from a list of potential results but beyond those originally anticipated. The question I want to ask is whether an architect can deliberately design in this sense without simultaneously regulating the new types of experiences that might emerge.

Citation

Mathews, Stanley. “The Fun Palace as Virtual Architecture: Cedric Price and the Practices of Indeterminacy.” _Journal of Architectural Education_, vol. 59, no. 3, 2006, pp. 39–48. [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1531-314X.2006.00032.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1531-314X.2006.00032.x)

M.N. Roy, designed by Emmanual Picault and Ludwig Godefroy in Mexico City, transforms an existing house into a grandiose, atmospheric underground nightclub while retaining the building's weathered exterior. The project is named after Manabendra Nath Roy, who had lived in the house, placing association between its earlier cultural history (tied with political history of Mexico) and its new cultural use. The architectural drawings reveal a carefully organized sequence of movement rather than a single open dance floor. The entrance spaces are intentionally compressed, with viewers forced to pass through them before approaching a large central room with a double-height bar. Elevated areas and curated lounges create an environment with a variety of experiences that all mesh together with the architectural statement. The interior exudes gravitas, with monumental timber patterns and volcanic stone, along with restrained but highly atmospheric lighting and acoustics, and a clear contrast with the unassuming street facade. The architects describe the project as an "anti-project," in the sense that they radically intervene in the architecture but simultaneously refuse to erase the original building.

Reflection

I discovered the M.N. Roy project while looking for nightlife architecture references on Divisare. I was initially drawn to the images for the interior architecture, which reminded me of the monumental compositions and atmospheric usage of material, like Pezo or Peter Zumthor's work. The more I look at the project, the more I find that the most interesting part is the club's relationship to the original house. Architects like Pezo design and build statements, pieces that feel original, striking, and permanent, new architectural objects. However, Picault and Godefroy work within a humble existing house. In that sense, the project reminds me of the idea of "subtractive architecture" I was exploring conceptually in previous blog posts: the architects simply refuse to impose a new image on the street view that exists.

This also reminds me of my reading of Kengo Kuma's essays on Tokyo and his call for a city made through less large impositions. M.N. Roy keeps a restrained urban presence, but in a striking contrast with Kuma's humble style, its interior is anything but neutral, with choreographed and thought-out plans and sections, and strong emphasis on intentional moments of observation and gathering. The architect restrains all the control they have for the building's public identity only to unleash it fully within the interior. A formerly domestic space is thus fully transformed into an arena for gathering, cultural meaning is completely transformed while the city's fabric remains intact. 

Simultaneously, the architectural heritage of Mexico is studied and reinterpreted appropriately: the thoughtful usage of materials and circulation inspires me to explore Korean architectural traditions in my project. Perhaps an interesting idea would be to inform contemporary spatial relationships with pre-urbanization Korean architectural principles. That is something I would love to explore in the future.

Ultimately, a conclusion I draw from M.N. Roy is that radical architectural intervention doesn't have to operate in a completely new form, and in that sense, architectural curation (rather than flexibility) could still promote new experiences if they operate appropriately as subtractive architecture.
