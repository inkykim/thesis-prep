
**Scholarly Article**

Summary

In "The Second Modern Condition? Compressed Modernity as Internalized Reflective Cosmopolitization," sociologist Chang Kyung-Sup describes a phenomenon of "compressed modernity" where sociocultural changes occur within a condensed period of time and space. The article is a theoretical analysis of this concept that includes sociological theory and comparisons between different forms of capitalist society around the world. In particular, South Korea is mentioned as context in Chang's discussion, as full-scale industrialization and modernization that typically occur over centuries took place there within only a few generations. 

This notion of "compression" occurs because modernization does not progress evenly: economic, technological, and cultural norms all advance at different speeds, both at a larger scale and that of families and individuals. A society can therefore become technologically and economically modern while continuing to operate upon social relationships of an earlier time. Rather than these older structures disappearing as new ones develop, there arises a coexistence of different stages of modernity, and corresponding tension between these conflicting stages. Chang writes that individuals must constantly negotiate with this overlap in order to be integrated into society. In that sense, modernity may not result in greater individual freedom, but instead require a more complex form of social adaptation.

Reflection

I'm particularly interested in the sociological theory in this article because it gives me a framework for something I've noticed, and been bothered by, while spending time in Seoul. Seoul is my family's home town, and although I haven't lived there since middle school, I visit around once a year. Every time I visit the city, its architecture, technology, fashion, entertainment, and overall culture and aesthetics seem to have changed. The rapid industrialization the city has gone through in the past half-century, and its current position as a global cultural hub, make it seem like it's an incredibly modern and rapidly adapting environment. Yet to me this modernity often seems to go hand-in-hand with an intense social conformity. The uncanny speed at which new trends can spread make their widespread adoption feel strangely uniform. A trendy restaurant or fashion item suddenly becomes ubiquitous, then disappears just as quickly.

This makes me question whether constant change is necessarily cohesive evidence of an expressive society. Seoul is highly dynamic at the scale of the city, but feels incredibly repetitive at the scale of individual behavior. Ubiquitous adoption is contradictory with independent expression. According to Chang's argument, rapid modernization requires individuals to become unusually attentive to fluctuating social expectations in order to remain up-to-date with society.

For my thesis, I want to study Seoul's physical transformation, and this peculiar relationship between rapid change and social conformity. The city was rebuilt and expanded at extraordinary speed, but the effects on its inhabitants and their definition of novelty and taste are more complex and intriguing to me. I am particularly interested in dissecting the distaste I have personally felt about Seoul's trendy, ubiquitous culture, and designing on an urban or architectural scale a third space that presents a "release" or "escape" from this social tension and conformity.

Citation

Chang, Kyung-Sup. “The Second Modern Condition? Compressed Modernity as Internalized Reflexive Cosmopolitization.” _The British Journal of Sociology_ 61, no. 3 (2010): 444–464. [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-4446.2010.01321.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-4446.2010.01321.x).


**Freeform Reference**

Summary

In "How Instagram Is Reshaping Korea's Urban Spaces," Michael Hurt describes the "Instafication" of Seoul, or the increasing overlap between physical spaces and the digital frameworks through which they are consumed. In particular, Hurt focuses his observations on areas like Seongsu-dong, trendy neighborhoods where pop-up stores and cafes are not only stores and social spaces, but also function as backgrounds for art and settings for social media.

"Hot Place" is a slang phrase used in Seoul that describes environments that encourage photography and social presentation. A cafe in Seongsu-dong might include a designated photo zone; a merchandise store might allocate more budget to photographable installations than to the products themselves. Hurt writes that the effect of such an environment blurs the distinction between physical and digital social life. Specifically, Hurt believes this phenomenon to be a social positive. Referencing Ray Oldenburg's idea of the "third space," a social spot between home and work that fosters face-to-face interaction, Hurt defines a new form of "n-th space" in Seoul that fosters communication in the digital realm that can engage with the physical space in a positive communal way.

Reflection

I found this article while researching the spaces of Seoul that had given me an uncanny feeling, and I am unconvinced by his conclusion. I completely agree that judging Seoul through a Western definition of necessary social interaction (as in Oldenburg's definition) would be counterproductive, but am skeptical that spaces designed around digital performance actually produce interaction with as much human and experimental value as Hurt's definition of the "n-th space."

The "photo zone" is particularly interesting to me because its purpose is to encourage self-expression (i.e. posting on a personal social media page) but it discreetly defines a prescription upon how that expression must occur. Someone using the photo zone can take a picture of themselves when thousands of other people have produced the exact same image, due to the established location, background, lighting, and pose of the picture. The result is not an individualized digital identity, but more of a standardized ritual.

As an avid rock climber, I've noticed a related phenomenon in Seoul's climbing gyms. Climbing is a technique-intensive and social sport, and as a result climbing gyms are some of my favorite manifestations of an ideal "third space." Strangers sitting in resting areas can easily exchange questions and advice about a climb, and social communication is fostered by the passion climbers share about the activity. In Seoul, climbing culture is defined by climbers filming themselves on tripods and consulting Instagram videos of other people that have completed the same problem. While the purpose of the space remains the same, an interaction that could have occurred between two strangers is instead reduced into a scroll. The Korean climbing gym is bizarrely quiet.

I'm interested in this difference between "participating" and "performing participation" in a social environment. Instafication seems to be one way Chang's compressed modernity has manifested spatially, and I want to continue thinking about whether this actually encourages individuality or simply provides a framework for how individuality "should" look.

Citation

Hurt, Michael. “How Instagram Is Reshaping Korea's Urban Spaces.” _The Korea Times_, January 31, 2025.
