## I. Introduction

Rapid urban transformation and economic development in Seoul, South Korea have resulted in a city compressed unevenly by modernization. Within a relatively short period, the city has undergone extensive physical modernization, while traditional social structures have negotiated their coexistence with this process. Now, under a technologically advanced and culturally dynamic façade exist spaces characterized by rigid social expectations and homogenized patterns of consumption. This is a contradiction that has arisen from tension between disparate urban conditions that are forced to synchronize under progress, and it deeply affects not only the built environment but also the ways in which people interact with it.

This contradiction is perhaps most apparent in spaces of leisure. "Third spaces" between the domicile and the workplace, such as cafés and retail establishments, are not only commercial destinations but crucial backgrounds for contemporary social life. Yet their significant degree of integration with consumer culture and social media trendification in the status quo raises questions about whether these environments truly encourage spontaneous interaction, or are actually engineered to prescribe reproducible forms of mass participation. When the distinct visual character or unconventional attraction of a space is architected for commercial virality, the organization of its occupants fails to revolve around anything more than a familiar pattern of consumption and display.

Within this context, the nightclub presents itself as a unique architectural opportunity. Historically, nightclubs have operated as sites of counterculture and experimentation, radical design choices, and artistic production and expression. What happens at night, a temporal realm outside of the urban fabric of the commercial day, accommodates activity that is temporary, collective, sensory, unpredictable, purely visceral in the moment and nebulous the morning after. The thesis is a design proposal for a nightclub in Seoul that aims to create room for social agency in Seoul by encouraging spontaneity and cultural expression not limited by prescribed experience.

The purpose of this paper is to provide background on Seoul's contemporary history and its current social conditions, the relationship between nightlife and architectural experimentation, and architectural perspectives on flexibility and participation. [Section II](#ii-compressed-modernity-in-contemporary-seoul) discusses scholarly literature on Korean modernity and the definition of social spaces. [Section III](#iii-nightclubs-for-cultural-and-architectural-experimentation) discusses the nightclub as a cultural and architectural precedent. [Section IV](#iv-the-architect-as-an-author) discusses architectural theory on spatial agency. Finally, [Section V](#v-conclusion) concludes by identifying gaps in the existing research and directions for the future.

## II. Compressed Modernity in Contemporary Seoul

Understanding the social conditions of contemporary Seoul requires research of the conditions that produced the city's rapid transformation and corresponding disparity between urban elements. Sociologist Kyung-Sup Chang in "The Second Modern Condition?" (2010) provides the theoretical idea of "compressed modernity" for this. The framework of compression arises from change in economic, political, and social fabric that altogether occur within highly condensed periods of time and space, such as Seoul's meteoric rise from the Korean War to becoming a global technology capital. Under such conditions, there cannot be a gradual and linear progression from traditional to modern society, as disparate historical and social conditions must coexist and ultimately produce complex and often contradictory relationships. 

Sociologist Hagen Koo similarly critiques narratives of linear progress in "Modernity in South Korea: An Alternative Narrative" (1999). Koo believes that a significant underlying component of Korean modernity's development is social struggle and conflict, rather than an unrealistically smooth transition toward a Western model of civil society. In that sense, historical traditions were never replaced by modern institutions, but reconstructed and reappropriated within new circumstances. Chang and Koo both agree that contemporary Seoul under compressed modernity is subject to simultaneous negotiation between different systems of behavior, expectation, and identity. Importantly, this negotiation should be understood as an ongoing process occurring constantly between competing traditional and arising social forces, with direct effects on the built environment and everyday behavior in such spaces.

Sang-In Jun and Meeyoung Kim in "Cafe as a Third Place and Modern Habits of the Heart" (2021) and Michael Hurt in "How Instagram Is Reshaping Korea's Urban Spaces" (2021) introduce a spatial perspective to this sociological phenomenon. Drawing on the history of cafés as third places, the authors consider how environments outside the domicile and workplace (and thus, spaces where gathering is driven by social interests rather than necessity) contribute to the development of individualism as well as social relationships. Jun and Kim argue that the mechanism through which social practice develops is rooted in the space, making the café a significant setting for encounters and relationships to emerge outside of institutional environments. Hurt's perspective expands upon this by identifying an emergence of social media-oriented environments in such settings, thus creating a higher degree of overlap between social behavior and performance-like elements of photography and consumption. Hurt describes this as part of a broader convergence of physical space and digital activity in a highly technological society. For my thesis, this raises the architectural question of when a space invites participants to invent their own activities, or provide a setting for predetermined performance, with the integration of digital communication having potential for playful appropriation but also a highly engineered social network.

Finally, Kengo Kuma and Yumi Kiyono's dialogue in "Change, Tokyo! A Free, Loose, and Open City" (2020) offers an architectural perspective from a related timeline. Responding to Tokyo's urban conditions, which include large, enclosed architectural monoliths developed in response to rapid urbanization in the Japanese capital, prolific architect and writer Kuma theorizes that urban freedom might only emerge from architecture that subtracts, into smaller and more adaptable spaces rather than imposing programs. Critically, these sources establish a relationship between social transformation and the architectural environments in which social life unfolds. Kuma's ideology proposes that new social environments might emerge not through the addition of new architecture; I believe that they certainly cannot emerge through the current state of commercialization and new digital forms of participation. The question for my design becomes, therefore, not simply how to eliminate commercial programming, but how to accommodate participation beyond it.

## III. Nightclubs for Cultural and Architectural Experimentation

Nightclubs are a fascinating opportunity to investigate this question because their history encompasses artistic expression, alternative social behavior and cultural identity beyond just the programming of music and entertainment. Carlotta Darò's "Night-clubs et discothèques: visions d'architecture" (2009) explores the significance of nightclubs within the architectural avant-garde of the 1960s and 70s. Radical architects were increasingly interested in technologies producing light, sound, and thus events organized around temporary media rather than conventional architectural form. Consequently, the nightclub became a symbolic testing ground for new architectural ideas around technology and spatial arrangements. However, Darò also concludes by identifying an important contradiction that arose in this early experimentation, which is that the freedom that the spaces strived to achieve were designed by the architect's increasingly sophisticated ability to control environmental conditions.

An interesting next step in experimentation is thus the study of how freedom can be achieved as the architect relinquishes his control of the programming altogether. A significant example is the club Space Electronic, designed by Gruppo 9999 in Florence in 1969, and described in "A New Film Demonstrates Why Gruppo 9999 ... Remains Relevant Today" (2023) by Alessandro Bava. The club brought together music, performance, and multimedia installation altogether in an architectural intervention of an existing building, with each program giving rise to participation that the space was not designed specifically to accommodate. In 1970, the club was turned into a school, and in 1971, the dance floor became a landscape featuring live vegetation and water. The project demonstrates how architecture can function as loose framework for activity rather than a building designed for a purpose. Its use of temporary lighting and repurposed materials supported this ephemeral identity with minimal architectural intervention.

Nevertheless, the distinction between the intent of the architect and the agency of the participant remains significant. Space Electronic was successful in creating creative production, but a great part of that was shaped by the artistic collective that operated it. The freedom to participate in an unconventional program does not imply an equal freedom to determine how that program ultimately manifests in experience. Contemporary precedents of nightclubs that practice varying degrees of subtractive architecture and curated experience can also inform design choices.

Amsterdam's De School, described in Mixmag's "Curates: De School" (2016) is a project that transforms a former technical school into a complex containing and nightclub, café, and exhibition spaces. Rather than reconstructing the existing building, the essence of the architectural design is in the maintenance of the school's industrial character and layout while introducing new programs. The basement's already low ceiling and spatial complexity was repurposed to create a distinctive atmospherical experience for the nightclub, and classrooms became art galleries with existing hallways acting as natural circulation between the spaces. This project successfully demonstrates the importance of institution: programming and access are shaped by the management of the space as much as the detail of the architecture, as seen by how the complex was able to accommodate such a variety of participation by developing around its existing structure.

M.N. Roy, designed by Ludwig Godefroy in Mexico City, described in Divisare (2018), is a project that transforms an existing house into a nightclub while preserving the historic and humble exterior facade. The architect characterizes it as an "anti-project" that resists conventional expectations of renovation by retaining the building's identity while introducing a highly intentional and luxurious nightclub as a dramatically different interior language. This contrast operates through transformation rather than expansion. Even though its program is highly authored and exclusive according to the architect's intentions, the agency of the participant is maintained within the historic cultural facade of Mexico City. As such, the open environment that emerges within a nightclub may or may not be established by conventional architectural gestures: the more important character is a careful treatment of social precedent and a subtractive approach to architecture.

## IV. The Architect as an Author

This complex relationship between architectural authorship and individual agency is explored through other precedents that challenge the architect's control over how space is used, outside of nightclub architecture. Cedric Price and Joan Littlewood's canonical unbuilt Fun Palace is explored by Stanley Mathews in "The Fun Palace as Virtual Architecture: Cedric Price and the Practices of Indeterminacy" (2006) as an adaptive structure intended to respond to changing activities and user preferences through an algorithmic system around modular and portable furniture and spatial partitions. However, the definition of mechanisms designed to regulate change were ultimately rigid restrictions on a project means to be as open as possible. 

Herman Hertzberger in "Polyvalence: The Competence of Form and Space with Regard to Different Interpretations" (2014) proposes an alternate criteria on which the flexibility of architecture can be judged by "polyvalence," a measure of how a space can accommodate interpretation without necessarily undergoing a change in definition. The gap that invites design is not between rigidity and flexibility, but between architecture that prescribes behavior and architecture that invites participation. Thus, the architect's responsibility is not to formally relinquish intention for the purpose of accommodation, as Price did with Fun Palace. Architecture that is meaningful and recognizable architecture is not fundamentally immune to evolution through usage.

Lara Damabi's "Lina Bo Bardi and the SESC Pompéia Leisure Center: A Case Study in Adaptive Reuse" (2023) examines how an industrial complex was transformed into a public facility while preserving existing cultural practices. Rather than imposing an entirely new identity, Bo Bardi responded to established patterns of occupation and extended opportunities for collective use. In this case, polyvalence was conjured through the participation of a community that organically gathered around the industrial complex. On the stark opposite of the spectrum of architectural projects, Seung H-Sang's "Sujoldang" (1992) is a residential project with a highly complex and intentional arrangement of courtyards, meaningless thresholds, and maze-like relationships between private and shared spaces. Despite its highly engineered architectural gestures, it brings polyvalence to its humble resident by offering moments of inefficiency that become gaps in its program for spontaneous contemplation.

As such, architectural precedents describe a huge variety of approaches to spatial agency: programming change, creating accommodation for multiple interpretations, reworking or rebuilding existing conditions. None of these guarantee social freedom: their significance lie in how they negotiate the relationship between their intentions and the actual autonomy of their occupants.

## V. Conclusion

The literature establishes a relationship between contemporary Seoul's compressed modernity, architectural approaches of social freedom, and the nightclub as an area of potential cultural and architectural experimentation in order to accommodate authentic participation. One limitation of the current research is in translating the ideas of polyvalence and flexibility explored in the precedents into the specific spatial and social conditions of Seoul. Specific research into how particular arrangements may be situated in the fabric of Seoul's current nightlife scene, or more precedents with direct presence in Korean history and culture would help close this gap.

Ultimately, a proposal for the design of a nightclub, likely as a project transforming an existing building through a specific philosophical architecture gesture will be informed by my research on social phenomena, circulation, and programmatic relationships. The thesis seeks not to relinquish architectural authorship but to investigate how architecture can establish novel conditions for agency without directly infringing on decisions upon how those conditions may be used.
